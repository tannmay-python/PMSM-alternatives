Alternatives to rare-earth permanent-magnet motors

Read the brief
Issue brief July 2026 Electric mobility

The question

Can an electric vehicle keep the performance of a permanent-magnet motor without depending on rare-earth magnets?

Permanent-magnet synchronous motors are common in electric vehicles because they are efficient, compact and capable of producing high torque from a small package. Their rotor usually contains neodymium-iron-boron magnets. That small component gives the motor much of its performance, but it also connects the vehicle to a highly concentrated supply chain.

There is no direct substitute that preserves every advantage. Wound-field and induction motors already operate in production vehicles. Synchronous reluctance motors are established in industrial drives and are being adapted for traction. Ferrite can retain the permanent-magnet principle with lower magnetic strength. Iron nitride is promising, but remains at the manufacturing scale-up stage.

The practical choice depends on the vehicle, its duty cycle and the manufacturer’s capabilities. A larger motor may be acceptable in an industrial pump but not in a compact passenger car. Extra rotor heat may be manageable in one drive cycle and costly in another. This brief explains those trade-offs from the inside of the motor outward.

Start with the machine

An electric motor converts timed current into rotation

An electric vehicle stores energy as direct current in its battery. The wheels, however, need a shaft that can turn smoothly across a wide range of speeds. The traction inverter sits between those two systems. It switches the battery current into three precisely timed streams and sends them through copper windings fixed around the inside of the motor.

Those windings form the stator, the part that stays still. As current rises in one group of windings and falls in another, the strongest magnetic direction moves around the stator. The rotor sits inside that field and is fixed to the output shaft. If the rotor has its own magnetic poles, induced current, or a shape that magnetic flux prefers, it turns to follow the moving field. Bearings support the shaft, the air gap keeps moving and stationary parts apart, and the cooling system carries away the heat that the conversion creates.

  1. The housing bolts into the vehicle and keeps the motor aligned. Channels in or around it carry coolant past the stator, where much of the heat is produced.

  2. Two bearings hold the shaft on a precise axis while allowing it to turn. The rotor is fixed to the shaft, so electromagnetic torque leaves the motor as mechanical rotation.

  3. The stator is built from thin sheets of electrical steel. Steel guides magnetic flux. Splitting the core into insulated laminations limits circulating currents that would otherwise become heat.

  4. The inverter does not send one steady current into the motor. It varies current through phases A, B and C in sequence. Each phase magnetises a different set of stator teeth.

  5. The rotor turns inside the stator. A very narrow air gap prevents contact while allowing magnetic flux to cross. A larger gap weakens magnetic coupling; a smaller one demands tighter manufacturing tolerances.

  6. In an interior PMSM, shaped NdFeB magnets sit in pockets within the rotor steel. They create a strong rotor field without wires or current on the rotor. This helps the motor remain compact and limits rotor heating.

  7. The inverter energises the stator, its field turns, and the magnetised rotor turns with it. The cooling jacket removes losses while the shaft carries useful torque to the gearbox or wheels.

From current to torque

Three timed currents create one rotating magnetic direction

A single energised coil pair would pull a magnetic rotor towards one fixed direction. That is not enough for continuous rotation. A traction inverter therefore supplies three phase currents separated in time. When phase A is strongest, the stator field points mainly along phase A. As A weakens and B strengthens, the combined field moves between them. Phase C continues the sequence. Repeating the cycle makes the magnetic direction travel around the stator without moving any stator part.

In a PMSM, the rotor’s embedded magnets provide a second magnetic direction. With no mechanical load, the two directions are nearly aligned. When the shaft must produce torque, the rotor direction trails the stator field by a small angle. They continue to rotate at the same speed. The controller regulates that separation while also limiting current and temperature.

Rare-earth supply concentration

A small rotor component connects the vehicle to a concentrated supply chain

NdFeB magnets combine high magnetic strength with a high resistance to demagnetisation. Neodymium and praseodymium are central to that performance. Some grades intended for high temperatures also contain dysprosium or terbium, although the quantity varies by design and manufacturing process.

Mining is only the first stage. Ore must be separated into individual rare-earth products, refined, converted into metal and alloy, milled into powder, pressed, sintered and machined into a finished magnet. The International Energy Agency estimates that China’s share rises markedly after mining.1

Motor architectures

Four established motor principles can remove rare-earth magnets

The stator, rotor, inverter and cooling system are designed as one machine, so the comparison below is intentionally simplified. Reference architecture

Permanent-magnet synchronous motor

The rotor contains permanent magnetic poles. The rotating stator field pulls those poles around at exactly the same speed. Because the rotor needs no electrical connection and produces little electrical loss, the machine can be compact and efficient.

What replaces the rare-earth magnet?

Nothing in the reference machine. NdFeB magnets create the rotor field.

Where does added heat appear?

Mostly in stator copper and steel. Field weakening can add stator current at high speed.

What has been demonstrated?

Broad use in current electric vehicles.

Magnet materials

A weaker or emerging magnet can preserve the PMSM principle

Ferrite magnets contain no rare earths and are already made at large scale. Their lower magnetic energy usually requires more magnet material, stronger flux concentration, a larger motor, or some combination of these changes. Iron-nitride magnets may eventually offer stronger performance without rare earths, but a useful traction magnet must meet several requirements at once: magnetic strength, coercivity, temperature stability, repeatable manufacture and cost.

Axial flux does not constitute a separate magnet material. It is a machine geometry that can help an engineer use space and active material differently. It can still use NdFeB, ferrite or another magnet.

Operating efficiency

Motor losses depend on speed and torque demand

Motor efficiency is the share of electrical input that reaches the shaft as mechanical output. The remainder becomes heat, sound and mechanical friction. A quoted peak efficiency describes one favourable operating point. A vehicle repeatedly moves through low speed, high torque, steady cruise, high speed and regenerative braking, so engineers study an efficiency map and the expected drive cycle.

Torque usually requires current. Heating in copper rises roughly with the square of current, so a hard launch or a long climb can warm the stator quickly. High speed raises electrical frequency and therefore iron loss. An induction rotor adds heat because current flows through its cage. A wound-field rotor adds excitation loss. A PMSM may need extra stator current to weaken its effective field at high speed.

Commercial status

Commercial evidence should be read in discrete steps

A production vehicle, an industrial product, a vehicle pilot and a material-scale demonstration answer different questions. The evidence board below records what has been demonstrated publicly. It does not turn company announcements into independent performance results.

Current vehicle production

Wound field Renault and BMW use electrically excited synchronous motors in production vehicles.34

Induction Induction machines are used in production EVs, including specified Tesla variants.5

Commercial industrial use

Synchronous reluctance ABB and other suppliers sell complete industrial motor and drive systems.6 Industrial use does not by itself establish traction performance at high speed.

Announced programme or pilot

Wound field Renault and Valeo’s E7A programme and ZF’s contactless I²SM are announced developments.37

SRM and ferrite PM AEM and Conifer publish development claims. Vehicle-level validation remains limited in public sources.89

Material scale-up

Iron nitride Niron is expanding manufacturing capacity. Public evidence does not yet establish high-volume automotive qualification.10

Where each route may fit

Application requirements determine which architectures merit testing

Choose an application to change the engineering question. The result is a shortlist for testing, not a ranking.

Requirements that carry the most weight

Low cost, compact packaging, frequent stop-start operation and straightforward service.

Routes worth testing

Ferrite PM, switched reluctance and synchronous reluctance can be relevant when material security and cost matter more than passenger-car refinement.

Evidence a buyer should request

Drive-cycle efficiency, low-speed torque, controller cost, noise, vibration and thermal limits in Indian ambient conditions.

Implications for India

The policy task is to build evidence and manufacturing capability around several routes

India treats traction motors and inverters as domestic manufacturing items in electric-vehicle support programmes.11 ARAI lists motor test beds up to 220 kW and provides motor and controller characterisation.12 These capabilities can support a more discriminating approach to rare-earth reduction.

Testing

Publish comparable motor evidence

Publicly supported programmes should report efficiency maps, continuous ratings, thermal limits, acoustic behaviour and complete test conditions. Peak power and peak efficiency do not describe a working drive system.

Manufacturing

Support the parts behind the motor label

Domestic capability includes electrical steel, precision laminations, winding processes, rotor balancing, bearings, inverters, control software and cooling hardware.

Market entry

Match the route to a realistic first market

Two- and three-wheelers and industrial drives may offer earlier opportunities for ferrite and reluctance designs than high-performance passenger cars. The test cycle should match the application.

Support

Release funding against evidence milestones

Laboratory hardware, a vehicle pilot, an announced programme and series production justify different levels of support. A memorandum does not establish manufacturing readiness.

Supply

Keep parallel strategies open

Motor substitution should proceed alongside lower-magnet designs, recycling and diversified refining and magnet manufacture. Each measure addresses a different part of the exposure.

Conclusion

Several motor architectures are likely to coexist

Wound-field and induction motors provide the strongest current evidence for passenger-vehicle substitution because both are already used in production. Synchronous reluctance has a substantial industrial base, although traction designs face different speed, packaging and acoustic requirements. Ferrite can reduce mineral exposure within a permanent-magnet architecture, with a probable cost in material volume or machine complexity. Iron nitride remains contingent on manufacturing scale-up and qualification.

Buyers and policymakers need a tested account of drive-cycle efficiency, continuous output, noise, cooling, control hardware, production status and material exposure. A single peak figure cannot provide that evidence.
