M.01 / Summary

Summary

Permanent-magnet synchronous motors are widely used in electric vehicles because they combine high efficiency with high torque density. Most use neodymium-iron-boron magnets in the rotor. The International Energy Agency estimates that China accounted for 60 per cent of mined magnet rare-earth output, 91 per cent of separation and refining, and 94 per cent of sintered permanent magnet production in 2024.1

This brief compares the motor architectures and magnet materials that can reduce that dependence. Wound-field and induction motors already run in production vehicles. Synchronous reluctance motors are well established in industrial drives and are being developed for traction. Ferrite magnets are commercially mature but weaker than neodymium magnets. Iron-nitride magnets remain at the manufacturing scale-up stage.

No single architecture suits every application. Vehicle size, duty cycle, cooling, noise limits, control hardware and manufacturing capability all affect the choice. Diversified magnet supply, recycling and lower-magnet designs therefore remain relevant alongside motor substitution.

M.02 / Motor anatomy

How an electric motor turns

Battery current reaches the motor through an inverter. The inverter times current through groups of stator windings, creating a rotating magnetic field. The rotor turns because it carries another magnetic field, has current induced in it, or offers the field an easier magnetic path.

Housing and bearings

The housing carries structural and cooling loads. Bearings support the shaft while allowing it to rotate.

  1. The housing supports the motor. Bearings hold the shaft on its axis.

  2. Thin electrical-steel sheets guide magnetic flux and limit eddy-current loss.

  3. The inverter supplies timed current to these windings.

  4. The rotor turns inside the stator. A narrow air gap separates the two.

  5. Neodymium-iron-boron magnets are embedded in the rotor core.

  6. Timed stator current produces a rotating field. The rotor turns in step with it.

M.03 / Three-phase field

Current in the stator produces a rotating field

Three-phase current rises and falls in a fixed sequence. Together, the phase windings create one magnetic axis that turns around the stator. A PMSM rotor carries its own magnetic axis. Under load, the two axes are separated by a torque angle while rotating at the same speed.

M.05 / Taxonomy

Architecture, magnet material and geometry are separate choices

Terms used in company announcements often mix these choices. “Virtual magnet” usually describes electrical rotor excitation, not a new motor principle. Axial flux describes the direction of magnetic flux and can use either rare-earth or non-rare-earth magnets.

M.07 / Permanent magnets

Alternative magnets retain the PMSM principle

Changing magnet material leaves the basic motor architecture intact, but alters size, thermal margin and manufacturing risk. A useful comparison requires several magnetic properties; saturation magnetisation alone is not enough.

Magnet material Rare earths Engineering effect Evidence
NdFeB Yes High magnetic energy supports compact, high-torque machines. Grade and temperature determine whether heavy rare earths are used. Mass production
Ferrite No Lower magnetic strength usually requires more magnet volume, more careful flux concentration or a larger motor. Mature material; traction designs developing
Iron nitride No Promising material properties must still be converted into repeatable magnets with suitable coercivity, temperature behaviour and cost. Manufacturing scale-up10

Axial flux is a geometry. It may help an engineer redistribute active material, but it does not by itself remove rare earths or recover the performance lost when a weaker magnet is substituted.

M.08 / Operating conditions

Motor losses change with speed and torque

Peak efficiency describes one operating point. Copper loss grows strongly with current. Iron loss rises with electrical frequency and flux. Induction machines add rotor-current loss, while wound-field machines add excitation loss. At high speed, a PMSM may draw field-weakening current.

M.10 / Application fit

Application requirements change the preferred motor

The table identifies the considerations that favour each architecture. It is not a ranking. Final selection depends on a tested design, the vehicle duty cycle and the manufacturer’s production system.

Requirements that matter

Low cost, compact packaging, stop-start operation and serviceability.

Architectures worth testing

Ferrite PM, switched reluctance and synchronous reluctance can be relevant where cost and material security outweigh strict passenger-car refinement targets.

Evidence needed

Drive-cycle efficiency, low-speed torque, controller cost, acoustic performance and thermal limits in Indian ambient conditions.

M.11 / India

Implications for Indian industry and policy

India already treats traction motors and inverters as domestic manufacturing items in electric-vehicle support programmes.11 ARAI lists motor test beds up to 220 kW and provides motor and controller characterisation.12 The next policy question is what evidence should be required before an alternative is treated as production-ready.

01

Publish comparable test evidence

Publicly supported programmes should report full efficiency maps, continuous ratings, thermal limits, acoustic performance and test conditions. Peak figures are not enough for procurement.

02

Support the manufacturing system

Domestic capability extends beyond final motor assembly. It includes electrical steel, precision laminations, windings, rotor balancing, bearings, inverters, controls and cooling hardware.

03

Match programmes to nearer-term markets

Two- and three-wheelers and industrial drives may offer earlier opportunities for ferrite and reluctance designs than high-performance passenger cars. Testing should reflect each application’s duty cycle.

04

Use evidence milestones

Laboratory prototypes, vehicle pilots, announced production and series production should trigger different levels of support. Memoranda and company targets should not be counted as production evidence.

05

Keep parallel supply strategies open

Motor substitution should proceed alongside magnet thrift, recycling and diversified refining and magnet manufacture. These measures address different parts of the same exposure.

M.12 / Conclusion

Several motor architectures are likely to coexist

Wound-field and induction motors provide the strongest current evidence for passenger-vehicle substitution because both are already used in production. Synchronous reluctance has a substantial industrial base, although traction designs face different speed, packaging and acoustic requirements. Ferrite can reduce mineral exposure within a permanent-magnet architecture, with a likely penalty in material volume or motor design complexity. Iron nitride remains contingent on manufacturing scale-up and qualification.

Motor selection remains an application-specific engineering decision. Buyers and policymakers need comparable test data, explicit evidence categories and a clear account of cost, efficiency, noise, cooling and material exposure.
